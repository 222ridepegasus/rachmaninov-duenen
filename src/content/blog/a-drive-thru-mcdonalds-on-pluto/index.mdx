This post stems from earlier in 2025 where I'd though I'd try GPT's 03-Mini model and see how well it did with its 'advanced reasoning' and 'deep research'. 

This then, is something of a pseudo-thought experiment, derived from the famous [Russel's Teapot conundrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell%27s_teapot) - and, it's a question that's no doubt on nobody's lips.

*Here we go then...*

---

Pluto, once our 9th Planet, was first visited by Nasa's deep space probe '[*New Horizons*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Horizons)' in 2015. This trailblazing mission revealed a frigid desert world of ice and rock, with plenty of geological quirks and surprises to keep planetary scientists busy to this day.

However, during *New Horizons* rapid fly-by approximately only 75% of the surface was scanned and photographed, and that got me thinking 🤔 ...

<div class={QuoteWrap}>
  <blockquote class={KieranTheme}>
    <p>Hello there, I have a question that broadly blends astro-physics, exobiology and philosophy.</p>
    <br />
    <p>I say to you that the dark side of Pluto has either one of the following:</p>
    <br />
    <ol class="font-serif list-decimal list-inside space-y-2">
      <li>A crashed soviet space probe of unknown design, it looks like it could be from the future, but that's, of course, impossible?</li>
      <li>An LLM housed in a rectangular obelisk buried just 1 meter under the surface. Upon deciphering it’s lexicon, it claims it’s creators were a reptilian species that originated from earth 160 million years ago.</li>
      <li>An alien probe, made of exotic materials we are completely unfamiliar with.</li>
      <li>A drive-thru Mcdonalds, frozen, lifeless and empty of patrons, but there nonetheless.</li>
    </ol>
    <br />
     <p>In the spirit of Russels teapot can you rank these outlandish suggestions and tell me if there is even the tiniest, quark-sized element of plausibilty here?</p>

  <blockquote class={NovaTheme}>

    <br />
    <p>The Fermi paradox (“Where is everybody?”) would be partially answered – they exist, but perhaps only their machines reach us.     <br />

<p>Well, that wraps up the most plausible of the four suggestions, as I noted, no real surprises or revelations. Onto number two, which I guess I derived as an extension to the [lost cosmonauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Cosmonauts) conspiracy theory.</p>

    <br />

    <br />
    <p>A “futuristic” Soviet probe suggests an anachronism – perhaps a secret project with technology beyond its time, or even a craft from an alternate timeline or future. Scientifically, the prior probability of this scenario is extremely low; it would require rewriting engineering history or invoking time travel. If such a wreck were found, more plausible explanations might be misidentification (maybe debris from an interstellar object or alien craft bearing a pseudo-USSR emblem).</p>
  </blockquote>

<div class={QuoteWrap}>
  <blockquote class={NovaTheme}>
    <p>Philosophically, this discovery would be deeply perplexing. It challenges our trust in recorded history and the transparency of scientific knowledge. One might ask: Could we have missed such a monumental event, or is reality itself not as it seems?</p>
    <br />
    <p> To entertain this, one might invoke Occam’s razor – the simplest explanation (that it’s not truly a Soviet human-made probe) would likely prevail. Perhaps it’s a hoax or an alien artifact designed to look Soviet (raising bizarre questions about alien motives).</p>

<div class={QuoteWrap}>

    <br />
    <p> As Bayesian reasoning affirms, “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” precisely because of the “overwhelming prior probability” against them . Barring ironclad evidence, our rational inclination is to reject this scenario.</p>
    <br />
    <p>In summary, while not physically impossible (in principle a spacecraft could crash on Pluto), the lack of any historical or technical basis makes this far less plausible than an alien probe. It’s a cosmic oddity that would demand we question how we know what we know.</p>
  </blockquote>
</div>

It entails a convergence of implausibilities: intelligence evolving in reptiles to a high level, advanced computing long before humans, and space travel capable of reaching Pluto – all utterly unknown to science.</p>

<p>So we arrive at the edge of laser thin credulity. Has o3-Mini Nova not watched Star Trek: Voyager? Where a race of advanced ancient reptiles, the "Voth," were depicted as leaving earth to settle on the other side of the galaxy. I guess not!</p>

<p>On a serious note though, and it's where this idea stems from, Professor [David Kipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kipping) recently postulated that perhaps humanity's best chance of contacting an 'alien' species is via this method.</p>

<p>He suggests that, humanity leaves a 'beacon' buried on the moon, much in the way that is suggested in Kubrick's [2001: A Space Oddessy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001:_A_Space_Odyssey_(film)). That beacon, which could be a monolith, contains an LLM capable of withstanding deep time (hundreds of millions years) and houses knowledge of all our culture and achievements.</p>

<p>The idea being that ultimately one day humanity will be gone, but perhaps another technological advanced civilisation might arise within the 800-900 million years of habitabilty that remain on earth. Should they achieve space flight, then they should be able to find and communicate with the beacon. it's a truly humbling, and not implausible concept. If a a highly advanced civilisation (us) has arisen once before, why not again?</p>

<div class={QuoteWrap}>
  <blockquote class={NovaTheme}>

In Bayesian terms, the prior for an advanced Mesozoic civilization is so minuscule that virtually no ordinary evidence would ever overcome it – only a discovery as direct as this hypothetical obelisk itself could do so.</p>

    <p>In short, the concept is almost self-refuting in the face of everything we know about Earth’s history, making its plausibility vanishingly small.</p>

    <p>The environment on Pluto (surface temperature around –240 °C, with only ~13 microbar atmosphere) would flash-freeze and preserve a building’s contents in permafrost . While that means a McDonald’s could *remain* intact if somehow placed there, the key problem is explaining how it got there in the first place.</p>
    <br />
    <p>No known physical or historical mechanism accounts for a 20th-century American restaurant appearing on Pluto. This scenario has effectively zero prior probability – it’s as implausible as finding a ceramic teapot orbiting the Sun (Russell’s famous illustration of an unfalsifiable claim).</p>

And this is where, things get extremely weird, as o3-Mini Nova goes full [Modal Realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_realism) on me, breaking down the concept of infinity and possibility.

<div class={QuoteWrap}>
  <blockquote class={NovaTheme}>
    <p><strong>1.  Infinite size ≠ “everything happens”</strong></p>
    <br />
    <p>Modern inflationary models do allow the overall universe to be spatially infinite.  Max Tegmark calls the ensemble of all far‑flung Hubble volumes a **Level I multiverse**—regions with the same laws of physics but different initial conditions.  In such a model, every finite arrangement of matter that has a non‑zero chance of occurring does re‑occur, and because the number of Hubble volumes is countably infinite, each of those arrangements appears infinitely often.  </p>
    <br />
    <p>But the key clause is “non‑zero chance.”  Boltzmann‑brain calculations illustrate the point: given enough space‑time, freak quantum fluctuations can briefly assemble self‑aware brains, yet the odds per cubic light‑year are so tiny that defining “how many” quickly runs into thorny measure questions. A McDonald’s on Pluto is vastly more complex than a brain blob, so the raw fluctuation probability is astronomically lower.</p>
  </blockquote>
</div>

We're back on the ropes here...

<div class={QuoteWrap}>

  </blockquote>
